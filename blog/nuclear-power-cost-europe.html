How much does nuclear power cost? 70 years of European reactors in one chart

In social media debates on nuclear versus renewables I see a lot of slogans and few numbers. So we tried to put two key facts about nuclear power into a single chart: how long each European reactor took to build, and how much its electricity costs. Here is what the chart shows, and what it means if Italy decides to invest in nuclear alongside solar and wind.

What the chart shows

The chart covers 288 reactors with 222 GW of capacity that started commercial operation in Europe between 1954 and 2024. For each one it shows the levelised cost of electricity (LCOE): the average cost of every megawatt-hour the plant produces over its lifetime, counting construction, financing, fuel, operation and maintenance. All values are in 2024 euros and assume the same 7% cost of capital, so plants from different decades can be compared on one scale.

One reactor is off the scale: the French fast breeder Superphénix, at about 3,294 €/MWh.

1. The cost of nuclear power depends on when the plant was built

The LCOE varies a lot with the year of construction. The first reactors of the 1950s and early 1960s cost 150 to 200 €/MWh. The median then falls steeply, and the reactors that came online in the 1970s and 1980s mostly sit between about 50 and 70 €/MWh. From the 1990s the median rises again, to around 70 €/MWh in the 2000s and 2010s, and in the last few years it climbs above 100 €/MWh.

There are still few recent plants, so it is hard to draw solid statistical conclusions from them. The direction is clear, though: the reactors built most recently are the most expensive.

2. Since 1995, no reactor has matched today's solar and wind

The three most recent large reactors, Flamanville 3 in France, Olkiluoto 3 in Finland and Mochovce 3 in Slovakia, sit between roughly 140 and over 200 €/MWh. Someone might call them exceptional cases, but even leaving them out, no reactor that started operating in Europe from 1995 onwards has an LCOE below about 50 €/MWh, which is the level solar PV and wind reach today.

3. New reactors take longer to build

The colours tell the second story. Most reactors of the 1960s and 1970s are green, built in four to eight years. After 1995 most circles are orange or red, meaning around ten years or more, and often over twelve. Stricter safety requirements may be part of the reason, but the data remain.

This is why averages can mislead. An average construction time over all 288 reactors mixes the fast builds of the 1970s with the long ones of today and hides the recent trend. The same goes for cost: an average LCOE over the whole history of nuclear power does not represent what a plant built in recent years has cost.

What the chart does not show

LCOE compares the cost of producing a megawatt-hour. It does not include the cost of integrating variable renewables into the grid, such as storage and network reinforcement, or the value of a plant that can produce on demand. Answering that needs a whole-system view, which is what energy system models are for. The chart is also built on estimates with a single cost of capital: a lower interest rate lowers the LCOE of every capital-intensive technology, nuclear, solar and wind alike.

What this means for nuclear power in Italy

If Italy decides to invest in nuclear power together with solar and wind, these limits and the most recent data should be part of the decision: costs that have risen for the latest reactors, and construction times of a decade or more. Planning on the basis of historical averages would underestimate both.

Frequently asked questions

What is the levelised cost of electricity (LCOE)?

The levelised cost of electricity is the average cost of each megawatt-hour a power plant produces over its lifetime, counting construction, financing, fuel, operation and maintenance. It is expressed in euros per MWh and depends strongly on the cost of capital, which is why long construction times push it up.

Is nuclear power more expensive than solar and wind?

Per megawatt-hour, in Europe it has been since the mid-1990s. In our dataset of 288 reactors, none that started operating after 1995 has a levelised cost below about 50 €/MWh, the level solar and wind reach today, and the three most recent large reactors cost well over 100 €/MWh. LCOE does not include the cost of integrating variable renewables, so a fair comparison also needs a whole-system view.

How long does it take to build a nuclear power plant in Europe?

It depends a lot on the period. Many reactors of the 1960s and 1970s were built in four to eight years. Most reactors that started operating after 1995 took around ten years or more, and several took more than twelve.
